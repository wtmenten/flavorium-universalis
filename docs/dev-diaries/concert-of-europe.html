Dev Diary: The Concert of Europe

June 16, 2026

Published: 2026-06-16

The Balance of Power gains a parliament, and a peace you keep playing.

Dev Note: when I first shipped the Balance of Power, a coalition victory just switched off the situation and handed out a modifier called "the Concert of Europe." That left the best part of the nineteenth century on the table: the years after Napoleon, when the powers who beat him sat down and tried to run the continent by committee. This update makes that part real. The coalition can now act as a body before the war is won, and the peace it wins is something you govern.

The original Balance of Power was a contest between two blocs and a single number between them. This update keeps that contest but gives both sides an institution to argue inside of, and it turns the quiet "Concert of Europe" ending into a playable directorate. It adds two new parliaments, a reworked reckoning, and a peace that can outlast its own founding.

The Balancing Congress

Until now, the chief balancer acted alone. Every coalition lever was a unilateral decision spent from the bloc's pool of Resolve. That still exists, but the coalition can now also convene a congress and put its boldest measures to the whole membership. These are not the leader's orders; they are motions, and a simple majority of the members must carry them.

The motions are drawn straight from the alliance statecraft of the coalitions that brought down Napoleon:

  • Coalition Subsidies (Pitt's gold): the members pool a common war-chest in the leader's hands, and the lesser powers are propped up for the campaign.
  • The Treaty of Chaumont: a binding pledge of no separate peace. While it holds, the bloc is steadier, and a deserter still earns the lasting stigma of a defector.
  • The Pragmatic Army: a unified command for the duration of the war, and a real fighting bonus across every member.
  • A Joint Démarche: a collective ultimatum to the aspirant. The public rebuke drags the momentum of the contest toward the coalition and sours every member's standing with the hegemon.
  • The Partition Treaty: the powers agree their war aims in advance. The prospect of gain binds the bloc and steels its resolve for the reckoning.

How a court votes depends on where it sits. A great power footing the bill is cautious; a small power seeking protection is eager. As the Preponderance Share climbs and a hegemon looms, even the reluctant fall into line. Nothing concentrates a congress like a rising power across the border.

The reckoning, reworked

The old end-game could feel like a coin-flip at the finish line. It has been rebuilt around three clear roads, each tracked by its own counter, and each living in its own band of the Preponderance Share so they can never advance at the same time:

  • Hegemony: the aspirant holds preponderance long enough that a reckoning is primed.
  • A Coalition victory: the balancing bloc stays ascendant long enough to force the issue the other way.
  • Stagnation: the contest goes nowhere, the aspirant is eclipsed, and the whole drama quietly winds down.

The key change: hegemony and a coalition victory no longer win the situation outright when their counter fills. Instead they prime a decisive final war, freezing every other road until that war is settled. The outcome is read off the real treaty. If the aspirant prevails, the bid for mastery succeeds; if the coalition wins or fights the aspirant to a standstill, the bid is denied and the struggle resumes. The end of the Balance of Power is now something you fight for on the map, not something the situation decides for you.

The Concert of Europe

Win as the coalition with the right policy in force, and the situation does not just switch off and hand you a modifier. It founds the Concert of Europe, a great-power directorate that the victors govern, congress by congress, long after the guns fall silent.

The Concert is a closed club. There is no walking in off the street. The strongest victors take the founding seats, the chief balancer becomes the founding president, and the defeated former hegemon is readmitted only a generation or two later. Everything else is decided around the table.

An elected presidency

The chair is not for life. The presidency rotates by election among the members on a timer, and a discontented membership can table a Vote of No Confidence to unseat the sitting president on demand and elect a new one. (The chair speaks with two voices in any vote; every other member with one.) New powers join only when the congress votes to admit them, and the strongest eligible outsider takes the seat.

Three questions of state

The powers govern through three standing laws, each voted in congress:

  • The Doctrine: the animating principle of the age. Classic Balance of Power (keep any one power from dominating, and keep talking), the legitimist Principle of Intervention (the Holy Alliance: suppress revolution and shore up the thrones, at the cost of friction with the liberal and the restless), or Liberal Internationalism (Canningite non-intervention reaching toward Wilsonian self-determination and free commerce).
  • Collective Security: from concerts convened only as need arises, up to a binding Mutual Guarantee where an attack on one is an attack on all, and no member may make war on another without just cause.
  • The Congress System: whether the Concert sits as a Standing Congress, the permanent apparatus that unlocks collective mediation.

Conservative monarchies push for intervention; republics recoil and reach for liberalism. The small powers want the guarantee; the strongest are warier of the obligation. The Concert's politics are the politics of its members.

Acting in concert

With a standing congress in session, the powers can do more than legislate. They can reach into the affairs of Europe:

  • Congress Mediation: impose a general peace on a member's wars against outsiders, the great achievement of the historical Congress System.
  • Collective Intervention: a coordinated show of force to uphold the settled order, stronger where the powers have embraced the Principle of Intervention.
  • The Eastern Question: the powers manage the partition of a declining sphere, a Victorian set-piece that rewards the chair for stewarding the settlement.

Not a tombstone

The first roads (hegemony, the eastern deluge, revolution, stagnation) settle the question for good. The Concert is different. Even a well-managed peace eventually frays, so the Concert is built to outlive its own founding. A generation on, if the balance is threatened again, the whole contest can reignite with fresh poles and fresh ambitions. The peace was never going to last forever.
